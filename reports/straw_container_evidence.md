# Straw/container evidence for functional-geometry optimization

**Role:** straw/container evidence specialist

**Access date for all web sources:** **2026-09-24**

**Intended use:** source-verified handoff for a joint container–accessory optimization case in a Journal of Cleaner Production project

**Evidence rule:** observed or measured values are separated from manufacturer specifications, standards-derived constraints, calculations, assumptions, and unresolved uncertainty.

## 1. Bottom line

1. Public evidence supports several **real commercial straw configurations**, including a 100 mm × 5 mm straw modelled with a 200 mL aseptic carton, a 98 mm folded/152 mm extended PP U-straw specified for 200 mL applications, and a 102 mm/165 mm telescopic paper straw for small carton packs. These are documented reference configurations, **not proven inefficient baselines**.

   Sources: Tetra Pak/thinkstep 2019, https://www.tetrapak.com/content/dam/tetrapak/publicweb/gb/en/sustainability/documents/LCA-paper-plastic-straws.pdf; Baron SP 200, https://baron-egypt.com/files/data-sheet/200ml-Compi-block-Specification-U-straw.pdf; SIG 2021, https://api.sig.biz/media/x53pngzw/sig-paper-straw-portfolio-en.pdf.

2. The evidence is sufficient to formulate a joint optimization problem, establish manufacturing and usability constraints, and construct transparent material/packaging scenarios. It is **not sufficient to calculate a defensible minimum straw length**, because public sources reviewed here do not jointly provide container external/internal dimensions, straw-hole coordinates, required residual-liquid performance, acceptable mouth protrusion, and angle-dependent access for any one configuration. No diagonal rule or hidden “oversizing” rule should be inferred.

3. Straw material is only part of the delivered system. In Tetra Pak’s 100 mm case, the straw masses were 0.400 g plastic and 0.390 g paper, while individual wrappers were about 0.0735–0.0743 g and the 200 mL carton excluding straw/wrapper was 8.20 g. Therefore, a hypothetical 10% length reduction with unchanged cross-section and wrapper would reduce total straw-plus-wrapper mass by about 8.4%, but total package assembly mass by only about 0.45–0.46%. This is an accounting illustration, not a feasible redesign claim. Source values: https://www.tetrapak.com/content/dam/tetrapak/publicweb/gb/en/sustainability/documents/LCA-paper-plastic-straws.pdf.

4. Paper/plastic environmental results are **category-, geography-, boundary-, and assumption-dependent**. Tetra Pak’s European carton study favors paper in climate change and non-renewable energy but favors plastic in acidification, eutrophication, photochemical ozone formation, and water scarcity. Brazil and South Africa studies reach different comparative results because functional units, feedstocks, washing, imports, leakage, degradability, end-of-life, and marine-litter treatment differ. No universal “paper is better” or “plastic is better” conclusion is supported. Sources: https://www.tetrapak.com/content/dam/tetrapak/publicweb/gb/en/sustainability/documents/LCA-paper-plastic-straws.pdf; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.123070; https://doi.org/10.1007/s11367-020-01786-w; https://doi.org/10.3390/pr9061007.

5. Selected commercial paper straws can lose substantial compressive strength and gain liquid mass during laboratory exposure. Gutierrez et al. measured 70–90% compressive-strength losses in less than 30 min and approximately 30% mass uptake after 30 min for the evaluated paper products; higher liquid temperature generally worsened strength. These are product-specific controlled tests, not a universal failure rate or direct estimate of user replacement. Source: https://doi.org/10.15376/biores.14.4.8345-8363.

6. PFAS has been detected in multiple straw market studies, especially in sampled paper/plant-based products, but the studies do not justify “all paper straws contain PFAS.” Detection must remain separate from intentional addition, beverage migration, human exposure, health risk, and regulatory noncompliance. Sources: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2021.130238; https://doi.org/10.1080/19440049.2023.2240908; https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-025-37075-y.

7. PFAS should be a secondary discussion. The geometry case remains valid if the PFAS section is deleted: the central claim is that accessory and container geometry should be optimized against measured functional requirements before impacts are compared.

### 3.3 Standards-derived PP constraints

ISO 18188:2016 applies to polypropylene drinking straws with inner diameters from 3 to 12 mm. The official catalogue reports that it was published in 2016 and reviewed/confirmed in 2021. Official catalogue: https://www.iso.org/standard/61726.html. Public preview: https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/61726/3ede8ed7c91042edaf95570a82d77046/ISO-18188-2016.pdf.

Verified preview requirements:

- inner diameter tolerance: ±0.3 mm from nominal;
- length tolerance: ±3 mm for nominal length ≤250 mm and ±5 mm above 250 mm;
- wall thickness: at least 2% of nominal diameter;
- maximum-minus-minimum wall thickness: <0.05 mm, excluding color stripes;
- bend test: bend 90° and release at five points; the straw must not rupture;
- the standard also contains heat- and cold-endurance tests and definitions for sharp-tip film-piercing and concentric extendable straws.

These are **not universal paper-straw requirements**, and the 90° test is a rupture test rather than a measured wet buckling force or user-usability threshold.

No reviewed source supplies cartons per pallet or pallets per vehicle for these products.

For optimization, calculate at minimum:

\[
V_{\text{allocated},i}=\frac{L_{\text{carton}}W_{\text{carton}}H_{\text{carton}}}{N_{\text{straws}}}
\]

\[
\mathrm{FTD}_i=\frac{N_{\text{straws}}\;P(\text{successful serving})}{V_{\text{carton}}}
\]

where FTD is functional transport density. Model mass-constrained and volume-constrained transport separately; do not infer transport-emission savings directly from straw mass savings.

## 5. Functional and mechanical constraints

### 5.1 Wet-strength evidence

Gutierrez et al. tested selected commercial straws under controlled conditions. Water was tested at initial temperatures of 0, 21, 48, and 82°C; a cold carbonated beverage was also tested at 0°C; liquid height was fixed at two-thirds of straw height. Compression was measured in axial and radial configurations. Source: https://doi.org/10.15376/biores.14.4.8345-8363.

Verified findings:

- the paper samples were mainly hardwood fibers and used hydrophobic sizing;
- initial water contact angles were 102–125° for the paper samples;
- paper products gained liquid mass nearly immediately and commonly gained about 30–50% within 60 min;
- the paper products lost about 80–90% of strength within 30 min under the tested conditions, with some tests described more broadly as 70–90% loss;
- increased liquid temperature generally reduced remaining compressive strength; some samples lost structural integrity at 82°C;
- the plastic samples showed negligible liquid mass gain and did not show the same wet-strength decline.

**Interpretation boundary:** these tests establish time/temperature-dependent mechanical degradation for the sampled products. They do not establish a population-wide paper-straw failure probability, beverage completion rate, replacement rate, or acceptability threshold. Those quantities require use-specific validation.

### 5.2 Constraint set for joint optimization

The model should be:

\[
\min_{G,p,q,\theta,d,t,L_s} M_{\text{system}}
\]

or, when defensible environmental coefficients exist,

\[
\min_{G,p,q,\theta,d,t,L_s} E_{\text{system}}
\]

subject to:

1. **Liquid access:** the straw axis/opening must reach the predefined target region under allowed container orientation.
2. **Residual liquid:** measured residual after a standardized use protocol must not exceed \(R_{\max}\).
3. **Mouth protrusion and grip:** external length must satisfy a predefined acceptable range.
4. **Insertion/puncture:** diagonal tip, axial stiffness, and membrane design must permit opening without unacceptable force or tip damage.
5. **Leakage/seal:** straw/hole fit must remain within spill and leakage limits.
6. **Mechanical integrity:** dry and wet axial/radial strength, bend endurance, corrugation performance, and time/temperature exposure must meet thresholds.
7. **Flow:** bore diameter and fluid properties must meet flow and inclusion-passage needs.
8. **Safety:** no sharp-edge, fragment, choking, or material-contact failures.
9. **Manufacturing tolerance:** dimensions must be producible within declared tolerances.
10. **Applicator compatibility:** pitch, band width, wrapper sealing, folded length, and attachment geometry must fit equipment.
11. **Packaging/logistics:** redesign must include wrapper, carton packing, rejection, and integer pallet/vehicle effects.
12. **User acceptance:** awkward posture, excessive tilt, bending, sogginess, replacement, and rejection must remain within predefined limits.

The public evidence directly supports constraints 4, 6, 8, 9, and 10 qualitatively or through partial specifications. It does not provide defensible numeric thresholds for residual liquid, mouth protrusion, user acceptance, or failure/replacement probabilities.

## 6. Optimization-ready reference configurations

### Reference A: 100 mm aseptic-carton system

Use the Tetra Pak 200 mL configuration as an **observed reference**, not an inefficient baseline:

- 100 mm × 5 mm;
- plastic 0.400 g or paper 0.390 g;
- small foil opening and tight fit;
- 8.20 g carton excluding straw/wrapper;
- 0.0735 g plastic wrapper or 0.0743 g paper/polymer wrapper.

Source: https://www.tetrapak.com/content/dam/tetrapak/publicweb/gb/en/sustainability/documents/LCA-paper-plastic-straws.pdf.

Missing before geometric optimization:

- external and internal container geometry;
- fill height and liquid-domain evolution during consumption;
- straw-hole location and insertion angle;
- required external protrusion;
- acceptable residual liquid;
- puncture force and membrane properties;
- observed user failure/replacement.

### Reference B: folded/extended 200 mL U-straw system

Use the Baron SP 200 as a manufacturing/packaging reference:

- target folded length 98 mm;
- target extended length 152 mm;
- target ID 3.85 mm, wall 0.19 mm, OD 4.23 mm;
- mass 0.41 g;
- 28,000 individually OPP-wrapped straws per 0.26208 m³ carton;
- explicit applicator pitch and band-width constraints.

Source: https://baron-egypt.com/files/data-sheet/200ml-Compi-block-Specification-U-straw.pdf.

This reference shows that supplied use length and packed length can differ. It does not prove that 152 mm is excessive because the linked container geometry and functional minimum are not public.

### Reference C: telescopic paper system

Use SIG’s 102→165 mm telescopic straw as evidence of an engineering response to pack-height/use-length incompatibility:

- 102 mm packed length;
- 165 mm extended length;
- 4.6 mm diameter;
- diagonal cut and structure intended to pierce a closed straw hole;
- small modification to existing applicators.

Source: https://api.sig.biz/media/x53pngzw/sig-paper-straw-portfolio-en.pdf.

The source does not give mass or wall thickness, so mass savings relative to a straight or U-straw cannot be calculated.

## 7. Transparent material-accounting illustration

For unchanged material, density, diameter, wall thickness, and end geometry:

\[
M\approx \rho\pi DtL_s,\qquad \frac{M_{\text{new}}}{M_{\text{old}}}\approx\frac{L_{\text{new}}}{L_{\text{old}}}.
\]

If the Tetra Pak 100 mm straws were **hypothetically** shortened by 10% while retaining cross-section and leaving wrappers unchanged:

| System | Straw mass saving | Saving relative to straw+wrapper | Saving relative to full package assembly |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Plastic straw + plastic wrapper | 0.040 g | 8.45% of 0.4735 g | 0.461% of 8.6735 g |
| Paper straw + paper wrapper | 0.039 g | 8.40% of 0.4643 g | 0.450% of 8.6643 g |

Inputs: https://www.tetrapak.com/content/dam/tetrapak/publicweb/gb/en/sustainability/documents/LCA-paper-plastic-straws.pdf.

This calculation demonstrates why results must identify the denominator:

- approximately 10% **straw material** reduction;
- approximately 8.4% **straw-plus-wrapper** reduction if wrapper is fixed;
- approximately 0.45% **complete package assembly mass** reduction.

It does **not** establish:

- that 90 mm is functionally feasible;
- that manufacturing energy changes linearly;
- that wrapper dimensions remain unchanged;
- that transport improves;
- that environmental impacts fall in proportion to mass.

### 8.5 UNEP 2021 contextual synthesis

Source: https://lifecycleinitiative.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/UNEP-D001-Tableware-Report_Lowres.pdf.

The UNEP report is a meta-analysis of single-use tableware and alternatives, not a straw LCA. It supports the following contextual propositions:

- material production and manufacturing are often major life-cycle contributors;
- bio-based material is not automatically lower-impact because process energy and location matter;
- reusable options commonly improve with increasing reuse, but washing can dominate water/energy categories;
- end-of-life, especially decomposition assumptions for bio-based products in landfill, can change results;
- policy should account for product function and local waste systems.

Do not pool its results numerically with straw-specific studies.

### 9.2 Required distinctions

1. **Detection:** an analyte exceeded a study-specific criterion.
2. **Intentional use:** requires formulation, purchasing, or manufacturing evidence; detection alone is insufficient.
3. **Migration:** requires a food-simulant or beverage contact test under defined time, temperature, chemistry, and surface-area conditions.
4. **Exposure:** requires migrated dose, use frequency, body-weight/route assumptions, and background exposure.
5. **Risk:** requires toxicological reference values and uncertainty treatment.
6. **Regulatory noncompliance:** requires the correct jurisdiction, legal product scope, analyte/total-fluorine rule, threshold, test method, and placing-on-market date.

### 9.3 Current regulatory context

#### Denmark

The Danish Veterinary and Food Administration’s current paper/board food-contact guidance states that, since 1 July 2020, paper and board food-contact materials in which PFAS have been used may not be marketed in Denmark. It describes direct and indirect use, a functional-barrier route where migration is prevented, and an indicator value of 20 µg organic fluorine/g paper (equivalent to 10 µg/dm² at 0.5 g/dm²). Current official guidance: https://foedevarestyrelsen.dk/kost-og-foedevarer/foedevaresikkerhed/emballage-og-udstyr/materialetyper/papir-og-pap.

The saved English translation of Order No. 681/2020 documents the original prohibition and says the Danish version has legal force: https://en.foedevarestyrelsen.dk/Media/638204383156890144/Danish%20Order%20No%20681%20of%2025%20May%202020%20on%20Food%20Contact%20Materials.pdf. Because later Danish legislation may replace the 2020 order while continuing the policy, cite the current authority page for current status and the order only as historical/legal provenance.

#### European Union packaging regulation

Regulation (EU) 2025/40 Article 5(5) applies from 12 August 2026. Food-contact packaging may not be placed on the EU market at or above:

- 25 ppb for any targeted PFAS, excluding polymeric PFAS from quantification;
- 250 ppb for the sum of targeted PFAS, with precursor degradation where applicable, excluding polymeric PFAS;
- 50 ppm for PFAS including polymeric PFAS; if total fluorine exceeds 50 mg/kg, supporting fluorine attribution is required on request.

Official regulation: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2025/40. Official 2026 guidance: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ%3AC_202603084.

The Commission guidance states that there is no harmonised EU analytical method for PFAS in food-contact packaging and recommends a stepwise enforcement approach. Whether a particular attached or detached straw is legally “food-contact packaging” requires product-specific scope analysis; the numerical limits should not be declared automatically applicable to every straw without that assessment.

#### United States

FDA states that PFAS-containing substances were no longer being sold into the US market for use as grease-proofing agents on paper food packaging by February 2024. In January 2025, FDA determined that 35 related food-contact notifications were no longer effective because the uses had been abandoned; the Federal Register notice states the notifications concerned grease-proofing coatings for paper/paperboard. Official pages: https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/market-phase-out-grease-proofing-substances-containing-pfas and https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-06/pdf/2024-31703.pdf.

This is not a universal statutory ban on every PFAS-containing straw or every food-contact article.

#### EU single-use plastic straws

Directive (EU) 2019/904 Article 5 requires Member States to prohibit placing listed single-use plastic products, including straws, on the market, except straws within the specified medical-device exception. Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/904/oj.

This market restriction is distinct from comparative LCA performance and PFAS regulation.

## 11. Required scenarios and falsification tests

For each defensible observed reference, compare only configurations whose data exist:

1. current geometry/current material;
2. optimized geometry/current material **after functional feasibility is demonstrated**;
3. current geometry/substitute material where function and boundary are explicit;
4. optimized geometry/substitute material only after both geometry and functional equivalence are validated.

Stress tests:

- zero, moderate, and strong replacement/behavioral compensation;
- no wrapper change versus redesigned wrapper;
- zero logistics benefit;
- mass-constrained versus volume-constrained transport;
- integer carton/pallet packing;
- higher manufacturing energy and reject rate;
- low user acceptance;
- high paper wet-failure/replacement;
- conservative mouth protrusion and residual-liquid limits;
- alternative functional units;
- PFAS discussion removed;
- multiple environmental coefficient sets rather than one universal paper/plastic factor.

Break-even for expected straw units used per successful serving:

\[
\frac{M_{\mathrm{new}}}{P_{\mathrm{success,new}}}
<
\frac{M_{\mathrm{base}}}{P_{\mathrm{success,base}}}.
\]

If baseline success is approximated as 1 and one replacement is used on failure with probability \(r\), a simplified new-system material condition is:

\[
M_{\mathrm{new}}(1+r)<M_{\mathrm{base}},
\qquad
r<\frac{M_{\mathrm{base}}}{M_{\mathrm{new}}}-1.
\]

This is a transparent stress-test equation, not an empirically estimated replacement model.

## 12. Future validation protocol — not completed evidence

1. obtain manufacturer keyline/CAD data or physically measure container \(H,W,D\), internal corners, fill height, straw-hole position, and membrane;

3. define candidate lengths from analytical geometry, including tolerance and protrusion;
4. test puncture force, tip damage, leakage, and spill under standardized insertion;
5. quantify residual liquid after a standardized consumption task;
6. record straw angle, container tilt, external protrusion, completion time, and awkward repositioning;

9. record unsuccessful servings, replacements, rejection, and user acceptability;

## 15. Handoff conclusion

The strongest scientifically defensible straw case is **not** “current straws are too long.” It is:

> Public product evidence shows that straw length, packed length, diameter, puncture design, wrapper, applicator, and container opening form a coupled system. A reproducible joint optimization can test whether material is reducible while preserving liquid access, residual-liquid performance, protrusion, wet integrity, insertion, leakage, safety, manufacturability, and acceptance. Existing commercial configurations supply observed reference points, but functional slack must be measured rather than presumed.

This framing supports “redesign before or alongside substitution” without inventing an inefficient baseline, overstating lifecycle benefits, or depending on PFAS findings.
